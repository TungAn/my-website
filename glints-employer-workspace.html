Employer Workspace Revamp

Reducing Fragmentation in Employer Shortlisting

8 min read

Company

Glints

Role

Lead Product Designer

Timeline

2025 – 2026

Platform

Employer web

On Glints, a large share of employer traffic still comes through the web, and for good reason. Screening means comparing many candidates, reading CVs and following up across jobs, which is work that fits a big screen, while the app suits individual recruiters and micro SMEs who reply on the go. So the web was the place to get screening right, but its navigation was legacy, and screening one candidate meant hopping between the table, the profile, chat, the CV and often WhatsApp.

I proposed a new navigation and screening workflow, led the design and owned the project with our PM and engineers, taking it from proposal to a working internal version in about three months.

The idea was simple. Keep the table employers already trust, and bring the profile, the CV and chat next to it, so one decision happens in one place.

A Layout Problem Was Hiding a Workflow Problem

Small screens made the dashboard feel cramped, but the real issue was how many jobs one screen was trying to do.

Small screens were the first sign. About half of the employers on the dashboard used small laptops, where the banners and top bar pushed the job list below the fold. But the research showed the real issue was what the page put first. The dashboard was built around company details, promotions and notifications, while the one thing employers came to do, checking their candidates, sat in its smallest element. Four of the five employers we watched skipped the main button and reached their candidates through the small numbers on each job card, because that was the quickest way in.

One Screening Decision, Five Places

Employers moved between the table, the profile, chat, the CV and WhatsApp just to decide on one candidate.

Watching employers shortlist made the bigger pattern clear. Screening started in the table, where they could compare what mattered most, like age, location and work experience, across many candidates at once. But no single screen held a full decision. Whether they trusted the CV alone or read it next to the profile, employers had to hop between the table, the profile, chat and WhatsApp, and lost the context every time they moved.

Employers found their own way around it, and that cost us a clear signal. A phone number only showed once a candidate moved forward, so many moved candidates just to reach them. The pipeline filled with connections that were never real shortlists, while chat was barely used.

Too many context switches and repeated steps slowed down screening and reduced engagement.

The CV Was Still the Source of Truth

Profiles helped employers scan, but only the CV gave them enough to decide.

This is also why chat alone couldn't fix it. Profiles were often incomplete, and some details didn't hold up, like a candidate who was willing to relocate on paper but not in the interview. So employers trusted the CV, and many needed it anyway to share with the hiring manager. That made the CV the real decision point, yet the product treated it as a gate. On the chat page, "Accept CV" came before the profile, so employers accepted first and evaluated later, the opposite of how they wanted to screen.

"I prefer to check the CV… a lot of candidates have incomplete profile data."

An SME employer

"The CV is more comprehensive, that's why I didn't rely a lot on the candidate profile."

The CV stayed the validation layer, so it had to be one click from the table, not one chat away.

Design Around What Employers Already Do

The redesign needed to reduce friction between employer habits, not replace them.

Glints had bet on chat-first hiring since 2023, and I designed the chat-first apply flow myself, so the easy move was to push employers further into chat. But the research pointed the other way. For daily decisions, employers trusted the table, and they used chat mostly to get the CV before moving to WhatsApp, where candidates replied faster. Our chat page wasn't ready to replace the table either, so forcing the move would only have shifted the friction, not removed it.

So instead of changing how employers screen, the redesign does two things. It gives navigation its own place, so each page can focus on one job and employers can move between hiring tasks without losing their place. And it connects the profile, the CV and chat around the table they already trust. We weighed four directions before landing here.

Two Structural Decisions

We chose structural changes over small UI fixes, because the problem was fragmentation, not layout.

Restructure the Workspace

Why separate navigation over header tweaks

Navigation gets its own job, so pages stop mixing features and employers can jump between jobs, candidates and chat as they hire.

Connect Candidate Evaluation

Why embedded chat over a floating widget

Evaluation and follow-up stay in one view, next to the profile and CV.

Moving navigation into a sidebar gives it one clear job, so every page can focus on its own. Hiring isn't linear, and employers can now jump between jobs, candidates, chat and CV Finder without losing their place. On small screens, the sidebar folds to icons, so the table gets the room back.

A Transition Path, Not a Forced Migration

Chat-supported hiring had long-term value, but employers still trusted the table for daily decisions.

This was the main trade-off. The long-term goal was to move employers from traditional screening, scanning a table and reading CVs, to hiring through conversation. But behaviour doesn't change in one release, and forcing it would have cost the habits that kept employers productive. So the redesign is the bridge. The table stays where screening starts, and chat now sits inside evaluation, so employers meet it at the moment it saves them a step. The research showed this can work, since one employer already preferred the chat page for working through candidates from every job at once. As chat gains the signals employers rely on, more of the decision can move there, without another big change.

Build a transition path, not a forced migration.

Built, Tested and Ready to Roll Out

With little time for full user testing, we de-risked the launch through internal validation first, then a controlled rollout.

The new workspace is built and has finished internal testing, through a cross-functional review, a bug bash and a final UAT. I owned the rollout plan and aligned it with the other product teams whose work it touched. It starts with new employers, who have no old habits to unlearn, then opens to 20% of existing employers, with the old interface kept behind a feature flag. We'll watch shortlisting and chat activity, and hold back if job listings, support tickets or CV acceptance move the wrong way.

Success means employers find the right candidates faster and finish shortlisting with fewer handoffs.

Reflections

  • Design for the real users and their limits. The web served employers screening at volume, often on small laptops and used to working from a table. Fitting the design to those limits mattered more than chasing an ideal workflow.
  • Reduce friction, don't replace habits. Employers didn't need a new workflow, only fewer handoffs in the one they already had.
  • Let evidence update your own bets. I designed chat-first applying in 2023, and when the research showed employers still screen in the table, the design followed the research, not my earlier bet.
  • Plan a platform change in phases. A change that touches every page is bigger than it looks, so next time I'd ship it page by page from the start and get each part to employers sooner.
